1850     1   18  SALISBURY           CONNECTICUT
After 10 h Aurora was observed, not arched; moderately bright, the 
luminous part forming an angle with the place of observn of 10o; 
at the top N.23oW, hid by the Tachonic mountain N.43oE. hid by a 
cloud or haze. A narrow patch of dark cloud appeared from behind 
the mountain. At 10.30 about half the width of the luminous part 
of the sky. Wind N. gentle breeze. No beams, streamers or corona. 
No effect on Electrometer. Magnetic needle was carried 3o W. 
(Annual variation at this time 9o W.) The whole disappeared in a 
haze a few minutes past 11 h. [SI]

1850     4    6  LITCHFIELD          CONNECTICUT
Aurora b. when first seen it appeared as a luminous arch, 
extending from N.W. to N.E. not quite reaching the E. hor. With 
the W. end stationary, it swept slowly over towards zenith, 
shortening in E. till having nearly reached that point, and being 
somewhat bent towards the S. in the centre, it entirely 
disappeared from the upper sky by contracting into a luminous mass 
in N.W. Below this arch several streamers of various size arose. 
One mass of light appeared in the E. resembling a lamp hid in a 
dense fog, moved a little towards the S. and disappeared. Several 
others similar, though differing in size, now elevated etc., 
succeeded, moved gently towards S. and slowly disappeared, 8 m to 
9 m afterwards more luminous in N. and N.E. with numerous 
evanescent streamers. Continued with some variation till 11 h when 
observations were discontinued. There was not the usual 
accompaniment of a "dark bank" along the N. but something like 
this in the E. through which the bright masses mentioned above 
appeared to swim, while the more elevated of them, in the E. 
seemed like masses of lighth standing out from a clear sky. A few 
small scattering fragments of cloud were seen in the N. and a 
single line of cloud lay along the N. sky, a few degrees above the 
horizon, from which (the horizon) the light of the aurora arose. 
Two small meteors were seen, one N.W. and one N. [SI]
[LOV]

1850     4    6  THOMASTON           MAINE
8 h Numerous floating patches of white light over a large portion 
of the northern sky, followed by numerous bright broad streams 
ascending from hor. increasing in number - the flickering and 
fantastic corruscations spreading in a short time over the whole 
N. sky, reaching from Arcturus in E to the Pleiades in W. all 
converging in Zenith, and forming a beautiful corona near the star 
Nas-al-asad (head of Leo) at 8 h 20', from which point rays of 
light seemed to dart in every direction. Corona continued about 
5'. The horizon and the whole N. sky continued brilliantly 
illuminated until after 9 h when observations were discontinued. 
At this time the wind blew a gentle breeze from N. [SI]

1851     2   18  RICHMOND            MASSACHUSETTS
The Aurora as seen at 10 p.m. was one of the most Singular 
phenomena we have ever seen: the preceeding day was rough with a 
high wind from the N.W. which however fell to nearly a calm at 
sunset leaving the atmosphere clear. AT 7 h p.m. the first light 
was noticed rising in a column from N.W. about 45o. At 9 h 30 m a 
bow of light extended nearly across the northern horizon unequal 
in the density of the light, being in part luminous and 
semiluminous. At 10 h It appeared to have reached acme of beauty, 
not a solitary star was seen within the arch and South of it not a 
Star obscured, on the South the sky was uncommonly blue and 
beautiful, the moon looked calmly on as if to enjoy this wonder of 
nature and the stars lay as quietly as if in a summers Sleep; It 
looked as if two distinct horizons had come together, one in 
peaceful tranquility so pure that no element could disturb it and 
the other arrayed in gorgeous gloom, congregating the elements of 
the storm - from 10 h the cloud and light settled back into the 
N.N.E. and at midnight scarcely a vestige of it was to be seen.

In autumn and early winter, when these lights are most common, a 
change of temperature soon follows which often runs into severe 
cold. Storm sometimes intervene between the light and the change 
but not uniformly. At this season of the year they are less 
observed if not less common, but such was the appearance of this 
light, that we unhesitatingly predicted a snow storm within three 
days. The Storm came and although it commenced with hail it was 
not a snow storm, for after a slight fall of hail we had a 
powerful rain within the three days following, and the weather has 
been mild until the end of the month. [SI]
[LOV]

[clipping from unidentified newspaper, no date:]
              AURORAL DISPLAY AT THE WHITE MOUNTAIN

                   From the Boston Transcript
              Crawford House, White Mountain Notch
                              September 8, 1851
Mr. Editor: Meteoric phenomena of such a wonderful kind were 
witnessed here last Saturday evening that they seemed to those of 
us travelling in this region who were fortunate enough to behold 
them to demand some public notice; and I trust you will concur 
with us in our opinion, although no description, much less my own, 
can do justice to this singularly brilliant and even appalling 
display of celestial fireworks. During the whole of the evening we 
observed the ordinary tranquil Aurora illuminating a portion of 
the northern hemisphere, and shining with a mild steady white 
light, but remarked no variation of color or form; and it was not 
till about half past eleven that the avant-courier of the coming 
exhibition appeared in the shape of a luminous band, stretching 
suddenly across the sky and oscillating with a tremulous motion. A 
gentleman from Philadelphia had proposed walking down into the 
Notch to view it by moonlight, and as we drew near it, the twin 
Titans guarding the entrance [sto]od boldly forth agains a sky of 
unusual clearness, while [word smudged] collected in the valley, 
lit up by the moon and stars, [part of word smudged]bled a 
sleeping lake.

     Having advanced quite a distance into the Notch, we reclined 
upon an elevated rock to contemplate the rugged grandeur of the 
cliffs, as they rose in the clear soft light, when our eyes were 
greeted by the above-mentioned phenomenon. As we lay flat on our 
backs, on a sudden, from the upper edge of the shining segment in 
the north, while the lower part grew dark, shot forth innumerable 
rays, like jets of liquid light, which preserved their form a 
moment, appearing like a resplendent diadem of solid diamond on 
the Egyptian brow of night. Oblong spots of brilliant light now 
sprang into view in various quarters, which, becoming gradually 
elongated, burst at the top, scattering masses of light in all 
directions. Soon broad shining columns emerged from different 
points in the horizon, moving slowly at first, then darting up 
with incredible swiftness - suddenly vanishing, and reappearing of 
increased brilliancy, eclipsing the light of the planets and the 
moon; now chasing each other in lighning race around the sky, and 
finally enlarging, after infinite changes of form, so as to occupy 
the whole heavens. 
     A universal undulatory motion, similar to the swell of the 
sea or the motion of a wind-swept field of grain, but more rapid 
than the dart of the frightened serpent, now proved the precursor 
of increased beauty and of the most truly amazing phenomena. A 
small pitchy cloud of irregular form appears at the zenith, which, 
as it were, kindles and emits tongues of flame of the most 
variegated and brilliant hues - green, purple, pink, golden, and 
violet - and streams of fire, shooting ina sinuous course, as when 
"Hell's standard-bearer"

               "From the glittering staff unfurled
          Th' Imperial ensign, which, full high advanced,
          Shone like a meteor, streaming to the wind,
          With gems and golden lustre rich imblazed;"
while all over the heavens cloud-like masses, flushed with the 
richest tints, like the g[part of word smudged]ing light on the 
polished steel, ev[part of word smudged] the twinkling of an eye; 

                [two or three lines too smudged]

          Ten thousand banners rising in the air, 
          With their colors waving;"

and sulphureous flames, seeming to issue from the mountains, 
darted from behind, resembling the bursting forth of volcanic 
fires.

     Such a scene, calculated to excite the noblest emotions, I 
never expect to behold again; it were worth a voyage across the 
Atlantic. It might be compared to a vast canopy or tent, suspended 
from the zenith, inwrought with gold and silver, rubies and 
emeralds, and shaken by a mighty wind. And it would require no 
vivid imagination for one, shut in as we were by eternal rocks, 
with the skies kindling over our heads, to see above him the 
fretted roof of Pandemoninium set with "cressets, fed with naphtha 
and asphaltus;" and around, gigantic forms reposing on their rocky 
couches; or to think "her stores were opened, and this firmament," 
sporting cataracts of fire, "impending horrors threatening hideous 
fall."

     Nearly all the colors of the spectrum were exhibited in 
dazzling succession, green being especially prominent, which our 
landlord informs us has never been seen here before, though red is 
quite common. Nothing was wanting except the hissing and crackling 
noises sometimes heard here, and frequent in high northern 
latitudes; the solemn stillness, however, added, I thought, to the 
sublimity of the scene. After the lapse of about half an hour the 
varied colors gradually faded, and a dim white light alone 
remained in the northern sky. The aurorae boreales of Lapland, as 
described by Maupertuis and others, are very similar to this 
display. The weather at the mountains, for two or three days, has 
been the warmest of the season - the thermometer ranging from 90o 
to 98o. Yours, truly,                                A. Russell, 
Cambridge
                              W.S. Rogers, Boston
                              J.D. Sargent, Philadelphia.

Aurora Borealis - first seen by myself at 7 p.m. - A remarkably 
brilliant band of golden light extended from East to West, 3 or 4 
degrees in width, forming an angle rather than an arch at the 
zenith, waves of light moved in quick succession from E. to W. - 
In a few minutes a part of the band in the E. midway from the 
zenith to the horizon assumed a very ruddy glow as if from the 
rising sun, and soon a similar appearance was seen opposite in the 
West. - Beams and rays of light began to radiate from the zenith 
in all directions to the horizon, so that the observers seemed to 
be placed in the centre of an illuminated tent. Gradually the 
hitherto regular appearance dissolved into streamers and ragged 
sheets of light travelling towards the N. - some seemed to be 
tinged with gold and green - The unusual phenomenon soon 
disappeared leaving a gentle glow especially in the northern part 
of the sky. The duration of the above was near 20 minutes. There 
was sufficient light to enable one to read (and almost cast 
shadows) - No observation made after 10 p.m.

[Note in margin:] (From a correspondent of the "Portsmouth 
Journal", No. 4.) This properly belongs to the 16th day of March.

On the morning of the 17th March 1852, the aurora was distinctly 
seen from the west to the east horizon, the corona of which was 
near the zenith, while streamers flashed up from the north and 
East. The arch was wide, the streaks of light of differing and 
varying intensity. The peculiar flickering motion of the aurora 
was evident to all whose attention was directed to the phenomenon. 
The aurora by day has often been described in scientific works, 
but though of frequent occurrence, it is rarely noticed. Its usual 
appearance being faint and indistinct, it passes for a light 
cirrus cloud. [SI]

1852     6   11  NEW HAVEN           CONNECTICUT
Yale College. Remarkable arch of aurora borealis, which, from the 
definiteness of its outline, the line of its direction through 
well known stars, and its perfect insulation from all objects with 
which it might have been confounded by different spectators, was 
peculiarly favorable for determining its parallax, and thence 
deducing its height above the earth. (Observers abroad who 
"accurately noted the time, and fixed the position of the arch 
with reference to the stars, so precisely as to admit of an exact 
comparison of their observations with those given below" were 
requested to communicate particulars).

     At about 9.40 the arch was complete, and remained nearly 
stationary, moving, however, very slowly to S. until 10 h when it 
began to break up. At 9.50 "the time we wish to name for 
comparison") its breadth averaged 4-1/2o, and it spanned the 
heavens in a great circle, from a point about 10o N. of W. to 10o 
S. of E. passing through the sickle in the breast of the Lion, 
between the stars Gamma and Zeta Leonis; the southern margin 
grazing Gamma, Leonis.

     Thence it extended eastward, passing about 15o S. of zenith, 
across the Northern Crown, its southern margin almost reaching 
Gamma, Coronae. Further Eastward, it passed 9o S. of Altair (the 
brightest star of the Eagle), and through the head of Antinous, 
the southern border nearly grazing Eta of the Constellation. 
Between the crown and Antinous, the arch bent Southward in a 
peculiarly graceful curve.

     A diffusive auroral light, with occasional streamers, rested 
on the Northern horizon, but the sky was elsewhere perfectly 
clear, and there was nothing present to impair the singular unity 
and definiteness of the auroral arch.

     By 10.30 the milky auroral vapor in the N. began to assume 
the distinct figure of an arch, which very probably may have 
exhibited, in a higher latitude phenomena not unlike those above 
described. At 10.45 the arch culminated nearly under the North 
star, at an alt. of 15o, and had a breadth of more than 5o. Below 
it was a slaty-colored segment of the sky, in which the stars Beta 
and Gamma, Andromedae, then close to the hor. shone with unwonted 
brilliancy.

     Great aurorae seldom occur in June; the present, although not 
of the very highest class in point of magnificence, is by far the 
most striking of any witnessed during this month by the writer, 
after an attentive observation of the Aurora borealis for 25 
years.       (Professor Denison Olmsted). [SI]

Aurora 6-1/2 - 7-3/4 p.m. See notes at bottom of sheet.
[at bottom:] A brilliant Aurora in early part of evening from 6.25 
- 8 p.m. At 6.42 (M.L) p.m. I noticed the following appearances 
which may assist in determining its elevation. At that time there 
were two auroral arches, one above the other, the upper spanning 
the whole northern sky, and entirely separated from the lower, 
resembled a rainbow in form, being of uniform breadth, and almost 
motionless. At 6.42 this bow passed thro the great Dipper, passing 
between the 4 stars forming the bowl of the dipper, filling the 
space as much as possible without touching either on its upper or 
lower edge, its highest altitude was I sh'd judge, 30o or 35o The 
arch below, and resting upon a dark base, just touched Benetnach, 
the last star in the handle of the dipper, with its upper edge. - 
The edges of both arches were not perfectly defined.

1855     3   12  BRANDON             VERMONT        
Aurora. First seen at a little past 7 p.m., at first pale, but 
became quite brilliant, showing a variety of forms. Recorded 
observations began at 7 Oclock 40 minutes p.m. Light arch of ill 
defined light near the zenith with bright streamers in N. At 7:45 
clouds of pale light in N. with streamers in N.W. At 7.50 Arch of 
bright light in NE, with streamers in NW. Arch near the zenith 
uneven - covers the 7 stars. At 7.55 Bright streamers in NE. with 
bright patch near E. Arch overhead recedes to N. At 7.57 Bright 
streamers in NE and NW nearly meet at the zenith. At 8 Oclock 
streamers meet at the zenith and pass a little to the South. At 
8.5 the whole northern part of the sky covered with luminous haze, 
to the upper part of Ursa Major. At 8.30 Light recedes a little 
further N. to the middle of Ursa Major. 9 Oclock Light a little 
below the pole star and grows paler. No clouds to be seen. At 9.15 
Bright spots and streamers in NE moving rapidly to E. At 9.17 Arch 
of bright light in N. with dark segment below. At 9.30 Belt of 
light low in N and NE. with rays streaking up from it. At 9.35 
Bright arch of rays moving rapidly with a waving motion, red at 
the bottom and white above. At 9.35 Streamers in NW reach the 
altitude of the pole star. At 9.40 Bright light in NE with waving 
motion, red below and white above. At 9.45 Bright rays in N reach 
above the pole star. At 9.47 A few pale streaks of light from N. 
nearly reach the zenith. At 9.52 pale streak continues in N. At 
9.55 light grows paler.

remarkable exhibitions were in the Eastern and northern quarters.

appearances called the "Merry Dancers" one perfectly reliable 
person, who gave me the most particular account of the phenomena, 
hurried from his bed under the belief that it was 7 Oclock - but

1859     2   22  PERRY               MAINE
Washingtons Birthday celebrated by a most splendid Aurora - first 
observed at nine p.m. forming a dark arch rising fifteen degrees 
above the horizon, with jets of light shooting up from every part 
of it. The dark arch gradually disappeared, the jets of light 
starting lower and lower down and gradually extending in width, 
till at ten PM they extended through 180 degrees of the horizon - 
At this time the extreme eastern jets assumed a brilliant crimson 
color. This crimson, starting from the horizon at the east, was 
about ten or fifteen degrees wide, (all the remainder of the 
meteor was a white light) and it commenced rising and gradually 
passing toward the north, till at eleven p.m. it had traversed the 
whole extent of the meteor and faded out in the west. At half past

1859     8   28  NEWCASTLE           MAINE          
A most brilliant exhibition of Northern light. At light in the 
evening a pinkish tinge began to overspread the sky, dim at first 
but rappidly deepening it soon became bright red and deepened to 
purple. It is impossible for me to describe it. Its splendor at 
times was so great that it dimmed the stars. The manifestations in 
some respects differed from any I ever have seen or read of. The 
whole northern part of the heavens were overspread. White, straw, 
pink and purple in turns. The South except a small dark arc over 
the South East about fifteen degrees high was the same. This was 
very dark and stars seemed dim through it and the whole segment it 
inclosed appeared the same. From the summit of this the columns of 
light shot up again all round so that the whole heavens were 
enveloped in a variegated mantle of red and white and purple 
commingled and continually changing and varying. All the rays or 
lines of light starting from the horizon in the north and from the 
dark segment of the South met in a point nearly ten degrees S.E. 
from the zenith. This exhibition began to decreas in beauty about 
eleven in the evening. During its most brilliant flashes I 
examined a magnetic needle and a dip needle. The both were 
unsteady the dip needle showed less of an inclination and the

1859     8   28  NANTUCKET           MASSACHUSETTS
Long before sunset on the 28th there were evident signs of aurora, 
and it proved to be a remarkable auroral night. In the course of 
the night every modification was exhibited. Several coronas were 
formed near the magnetic zenith. The most remarkable circumstance 
attending it however was its apparent lowness and nearness to the 
earth. We seemed to be immersed in it. Whereas by reference to my 
minutes made during the display 32 years ago this very day, and 
the first remarkable display in the present age and, in one 
respect, the most remarkable, a belt stretched itself from East to 
West, about four degrees in width, bright as the moon, which at 
the time, I demonstrated to be more than 500 miles above the 
earths surface, yet partaking of the diurnal orbital motions of 
the earth. Nearly all the newspapers from Canada to South Carolina 
gave the time of its first contact with Arcturus which being 
collected and all due allowance made for imperfect time gave it a 
parallax that would not admit of its being nearer than 500 miles. 
Several less luminous and less remarkable appearances of this kind 
have since occurred. I have reason to believe that no aurora was 
visible in this latitude from 1797 to 1814.
[The observer was William Mitchell, an Astronomer, and father of 
Maria Mitchell, a well known nineteenth century female 
astronomer.]

1859     8   28  CLAREMONT           NEW HAMPSHIRE
[Entry of September 3:] In the evening of the 28 of August 
theobserver noticed at about 7 oclock p.m. a remarkable crimson 
appearance covering the Eastern sky. This was at first attributed 
to the rays of the sun shining upon the misty sky. This colour

crimson pillars constantly fading and reappearing. At 8-1/2 oclock 
p.m. the north was nearly free from the Aurora while the South was 
illuminated with a corona of light extending up probably 30 [deg] 
from the horizon while a broad strongly defined band extended 
through the zenith from East to West. The Aurora at this time was 
white lighting up the earth more clearly than a full moon. At 
9-1/2 an unusual brightness was observed in the South the entire 
sky being of a bright crimson while from a point a few degrees 
south of the zenith radiated in every direction pencils or beams 
of light which reached to the horizon covering the entire vault of 
the heavens. These beams remained fixed or nearly so for fifteen 
minutes when they began to flash and fade then these disappeared 
and the sky was all flickering with sheets of white light which 
flashed in every part of the Southern sky. This Aurora reappeared 
twice or three times during the night but was not seen by the 
observer after 10-1/2 oclock pm.
