1819    10   12  BRIDGEWATER         VERMONT        

                         Aurora Borealis

On the evening of the 12th of October 1819 this Meteor appeared with greater 
splendor and exhibited more interesting features than I had ever before witnessed. 
About 7 oclock it assumed the form of luminous arches in the heavens extending 
entirely over from east to west. These arches were then three in number and 
parallel to each other. The lowest arch was in the north and its vertex about half 
the altitude of the pole star. The next encompased the heavens about midway 
between the pole star and the zenith, while the third was in the south, in the 
region of the celestial equator. The arches, though at first entire and very 
luminous, continued so but a short time. That in the south disappeared gradually, 
while the whole northern half of the heavens overspread by one continuous glow of 
light. The Meteor frequently reappeared in the south and continued for a short 
time, while in the north and east, its form and aspects were continually changing. 
It would sometimes concentrate at particular points, where its brightness was 
nearly equal to that of an ordinary parhelion; and around these points were 
exhibited all the prismatic colors in all their mellow loveliness. Its most common 
appearance was that of a concave semicircle, having the zenith for its centre, 
from which streams of light, resembling radii, extended to the whole northern half 
of the horizon. The motions of the Meteor were undulatory and very obvious to the 
sight, resembling the motion of a flag waving in the wind. The general direction 
of these motions, which were extremely rapid and frequently violent, was from 
north to south tending at the same time towards the zenith. The sky was clear and 
of a very deep blue color, except where it was overspread by the Meteor. It was 
succeeded on the 13th by a slight fall of snow and N.W. wind. I was at Bridgewater 
in Windsor Co. when I observed the above Aurora Borealis. [PR]

[Remarks at the end of 1832:]
The Aurora Borealis was noticed twelve night, and was at several times very 
brilliant. It usually exhibited the form of concentric arches over the northern 
part of the horizon. It was most frequent in the month of October. Showers with 
thunder and lightning were frequent during the summer months, but no severe 
tempests. [PR]

1837     1   25  BURLINGTON          VERMONT        

Aurora Borealis - January 25. This evening was witnessed
at Burlington a most interesting exhibition of this Meteor. I first
noticed it about half past 6 oclock. It then consisted of an arch
of faint light extending over from the north west and terminating
nearly in the east, the belt crossing the meridian 15o or
20o north of the zenith. This arch soon assumed a bright red color
and moved slowly towards the south. The sky to the southward of
the arch was nearly black, and but few stars were visible in that
portion. To the southward of the belt of red light, appeared a
narrow belt of white light, and the red belt increased in breadth
and brightness as it advanced southward. It passed through the
zenith about 7 o'clock. The light was equal to that of the full
moon, and all the objects from which it was reflected were tinged
of a deep red or bloody hue. Between the red and white belt there
were frequently seen streams of beautiful yellow light; while to
the northward of the red belt were numerous streamers of blue and
white light. The most remarkable belt was the red one before
mentioned. It frequently assumed the appearance of blood and
varied from 5 to 15 or 20o in breadth. By 8 oclock the meteor
had lost its most remarkable appearance. [PR]

1849     9    3  HAMPDEN             MAINE
At 7.30 the entire hemisphere was illuminated with a diffused electrical vapour. 
At 8 h a dark arch in N elev 15o extent N.N.E. to N.N.W. (magnetic bearings) 
throwing off feeble scintillations of white light, like a fringe, which were 
absorbed by a narrow belt nearly coincident with this arch but somewhat higher, 
and having no well-defined base. from this belt, a series of well-defined beams 
and streamers extended to the zenith, or rather, a few deg. S. of it, and 
supported a broad and well defined Corona. The most remarkable of these supporters 
were two, one in the N.N.E. and the other in the N.N.W. in the resemblance of 
eagle's feathers, with the points in the Corona, and the feathers resting on the 
eastern and western extremities of the arch. The Northern edges were smooth and 
well-defined, and the Southern serrated or jagged. Stars were seen through the 
serrated vapour, but not through the main shaft. A tremulous motion was seen, at 
frequent intervals, along the margins of all the columns and streamers. At 8.30 a 
thickening of the atmosphere, or dark vapour had been observed at the south, which 
now assumed a rather indefinite dark arch of 20o elevn. and manifesting very 
slight electrical action. At this moment the entire arrangement at the N. made a 
grand and magnificent movement to the S, sweeping along as if it would annihilate 
the corona; but as its head and points approached within the margin of that magic 
circle, they were seen to break into fragments, and with a whirl, as in a 
cauldron, melted down and disappeared; while all the supporters of this grand 
parade, took up positions in the S corresponding with those they had previously 
occupied in the N. The Corona maintained its position in the place of the dipping 
needle, without being at all disturbed by the strong and rapid movement of the 
subordinate manifestations. On the north side, black broken clouds were sweeping 
over the sky, and shewing the white light through their openings, faint and 
feeble, and presently faded. At 9 h a broad belt of light, about 15o, similar to 
the Zodiacal, and evidently originating with the sun, swept over the S.W. portion 
of the heavens, and imparted a deep, strong red color to the beams and belt of 
white light over which it passed, In just so much as it covered, leaving their 
upper and lower portions uncolored.

     Observer (Jedediah Herrick) noted this last circumstance with great interest, 
as it seemed to sustain his previously entertained suspicion that the red color in 
all auroral displays might be of solar origin.

     The shortening of the supporting beams and columns incident to their transit 
from N. to the perpendicular, was instantly and imperceptibly supplied as they 
passed on from perpendicular to their appropriate positions in the Southern 
hemisphere, so that there was no interruption in their continuity and integrity, 
from the Corona to the base, but at whatever angle they stood, their length was 
just sufficient to fill the space. This was the first grand transit of arches and 
auroral arrangements from N. to S. which observer had witnessed. [SI]
