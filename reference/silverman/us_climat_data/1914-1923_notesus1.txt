Notes:

General:

	Note that many states for which one would expect many auroral 
notations, such as South Dakota, Minnesota, etc., etc., generally 
have none in the early years, except occasionally for large 
events. One possibility is that they are too common to be remarked 
as special events. Another is that for some sections they were not 
considered of interest. In any event, a search of the weather 
bureau journals for these states is indicated for a more complete 
record.

	Washington, D.C. is often cited in both the Maryland-Delaware 
and Virginia reports. Have not checked on whether they draw on the 
same or different observers, and not sure how to do this. Data for 
Moorhead, Minnesota are sometimes also found in the North Dakota 
section.

	The aurora of March 7, 1918 was seen as far south as Florida, 
but the maximum aa for the neighboring interval was 105 on March 
8. There were hardly any observations in Europe -- possibly the 
war there had some effect on this. In any event the occurrence of 
visual aurora so far south with such a low aa is very unusual and 
bears further investigation. One possibility is of a very short 
duration, but intense storm, with timing of maximum occurrence 
such that it would have been preferentially seen in the United 
States. The magnetic storm of August 11-12, 1919 had a maximum aa 
of 230, but observations in the U.S. do not go very far south, 
only to Washington, D.C. and Maryland. Perhaps the timing of the 
maximum was inauspicious. The aurora of March 22, 1920 was visible 
as far south as Alabama and New Mexico, with maximum aa of 269 on 
the 22nd.

	In the following any information given in the originals which 
is more than the mere fact of an aurora, has been transcribed 
verbatim. Sometimes a table is given which gives the date and the 
number of stations reporting aurora on that date. The number of 
stations is an indication of the intensity of the aurora, and is 
useful in that regard. These are given below as, e.g.: 7th, 2; 
8th, 5; etc. etc.

Locations:

	Some locations are too small to be towns or such. Several 
states include post office addresses for such places. Some are 
listed below. Counties are given as part of the data in the 
reports. Some are listed below to help in identifying locations. 

	Several sources have been used to obtain latitudes and 
longitudes: Encyclopedia Brittanica; Getty Art Museum, Thesaurus 
of Geographic Names; and the Geographic Names Information System 
(GNIS), U.S. Geological Survey. Coordinates were not always 
available for the smaller locations. For these basically two 
methods were used. For some a post office address is given in the 
data. Coordinates for these were then used for the location. For 
all sites the counties in which they were is listed in the 
tabulations of data. Where no coordinates were available for the 
location or a post office address, the coordinates of the county 
were used -- these could be obtained from the Getty Thesaurus. 
Where this provided no datum, the range of coordinates were 
obtained from the GNIS listing of populated places in the county, 
and the average of this range was used. Coordinates for States 
were taken from the Encyclopedia Brittanica. These are useful only 
as indicating a more general sense of position, but not for 
detailed work. Coordinates were not estimated for entries such as 
the "Northern" part of a state. These estimates are left for the 
user.

THE AURORA OF AUGUST 26, 1916

	Mr. William Arthur, cooperative observer at Washington, C.H., 
writes under date of August 27, 1916, as follows:

	"By great good fortune an observation came to me. At 10 
minutes past 11 o'clock last night, on my way home, I turned to 
scan the heavens and as my gaze turned to the north the scene was 
fairly startling.. A magnificent aurora waved. Long, fine 
threadlike lines of pure white light shot up almost to the zenith, 
then curving to the east grew faint. At the same time broad waves, 
silver white, rolled up from the horizon of dense black cloud 
edged with the same brilliant white and undershot with vivid 
flashes of lightning: like the swell of water of a calm ocean, 
themselves growing fainter as they advanced, and dissapearing as 
they reached the feathery curve. Beneath this, or rather on the 
face of the light, seemed to move faint, dark streamer-bands that 
moved in an easterly direction at more speed than the white light 
producing an effect difficult to describe.

	Memories of other auroras pass before my mind's eye, all 
splendid in individuality, but this one was a spectacle to last a 
lifetime of memories in 20 minutes of existence."

	This auroral display was also witnessed by the observers at 
Cadiz, Fremont, Geneva, Marion, Put-in-Bay, and Tiffin. The 
observer at Circleville reports an aurora on the 29th and at 
Wickliffe on the 23rd and the 27th.

	October: Ohio: Writing under date of October 13, 1916, the 
observer at Cadiz, Ohio, Mr. H.B. McConnell, thus describes "a 
curious phenomenon in the sky" and asks an opinion as to what it 
was:

	Lat night just as it grew dark a band of white appeared 
stretching across the sky. It formed a streak from the northern 
horizon to the southern, and was about a degree and a half in 
width, possibly a little wider in the middle portion. The sky was 
clear at the time, and the phenomenon was plainly visible as the 
moon rose to meet and cross it. Stars could be seen shining 
through it. It was thin and tenuous like a comet's tail, and some 
people imagined that they saw pulsations along its length. It 
gradually disappeared below the horizon, it first being seen at an 
angle of about 70 degrees. I thought of the aurora at first until 
I saw it reached across the sky to the southern horizon, and then 
decided it was a curious and very rare cloud formation, as there 
might have been a strong north and south air current at the height 
of the clouds. The wind was south at the surface of the ground.

	(Probably an auroral display that assumed a very unusual 
form. -- W.H.A.) [Probably William H. Alexander, Meteorologist and 
Section Director.]

AN AURORAL DISPLAY AT CINCINNATI, OHIO

	The following delightful account of an aurora at Cincinnati 
was furnished by Mr. W. C. Devereaux, Meteorologist, in charge of 
the Weather Bureau station at that place.

	"A moderately bright display of the aurora borealis was 
observed at the Abbe Meteorological Observatory from 8:35 to 8:52 
p.m. August 25th. It was first observed as a low arc of milky 
white light across the northern sky extending from horizon to 
horizon, the middle of the arc being about 15 degrees above the 
horizon. The arc was brightest at 8:40 and gradually spread 
upwards and slowly disappeared after that time. At 8:42 a dim 
white patch of light appeared about 5 degrees above the arc and in 
the north-northeast. This patch increased in size rapidly and 
became quivering beams of pinkish red light radiating from the 
center of the patch. The vertical and horizontal beams became 
quite prominent and formed a well defined cross. The faint red 
glow gradually merged into the white glow forming the arc below 
and the whole disappeared between 8:45 and 8:52 p.m. There was no 
display of colored light in the northwest above the arc at any 
time. The sky at the time was clear but no stars could be seen 
below the arc, probably due to the smoke in the valley. Stars were 
visible through the arc of light and also through the colored 
glow.

		Illinois:

AURORA BOREALIS

[Observed at Springfield, Ill., March 7, 1918, by H. Merrill 
Wills, Observer.]

	An auroral display, probably the most brilliant ever 
witnessed in this part of the country, was observed from 6:48 p.m. 
of March 7th to about 10:40 p.m. The phenomenon consisted, at 
first, of a perfectly formed arch of pale green light stretching 
across the northern sky from horizon to horizon. This band was 
about 15o wide. About 7:30 two spots appeared simultaneously at 
either end of the arch, extending upward in pulsating streamers of 
light until a secondary arch or semicircle was transcribed across 
the sky. This arch moved slowly southward, its crown eventually 
passing through and slightly beyond the zenith, where it rested 
for some time. The extremities of the upper arch remained at the 
east and west horizons long after the crown of the arch had 
disappeared, and these two sections became quite luminous at 
intervals during the continuance of the display. In the primary 
arch the altitude of the crown was 35o, and the azimuth, 195o; the 
azimuth of the western extremity was 130o, and of the eastern, 
260o. The crown of the secondary arch passed through the zenith to 
a point 80o above the south horizon; the azimuth of the western 
extremity was 105o, and of the eastern, 285o.

	At intervals, streamers of soft vibrating radiance, the hues 
ranging from deep rose to purple, yellow, white and green, 
extended upward from the main arch in sunburst effect, and were 
most pronounced a little to the west of north. Fields of dull 
crimson came into view intermittently, the brightest glows 
appearing in the west about 9:50, in the east about 9:55, and in 
the north about 10:00 p.m. All of these roseate illuminations, 
which would subside every few minutes to again appear in increased 
brilliance, were confined to the region lying between the two 
arches. There remained but slight traces of the luminosity at 
10:30 and it had entirely disappeared at 10:42 p.m. Telegraph 
wires running north and south were noticeably affected during the 
display, but not those running east and west. The wires would 
alternately go out of commission and then return to normal.

		Indiana: 

	Aurora of March 7, 1918.-- A very brilliant aurora was seen 
in all parts of the State during the evening and night of the 7th. 
Numerous descriptions were received from observers over the State, 
of which extracts taken from the accounts furnished by Prof. T.P. 
Irving, observer at Notre Dame, and Mr. J.F. Bradley, observer at 
Valparaiso, are fairly representative: The aurora began about 7 
p.m. with a pinkish-yellow glow and, rising toward the zenith, by 
7:20 p.m. had developed an arch which was about 60 degrees above 
the horizon. By 7:30 p.m. three areas of white lights had formed 
in the south, the center one being very vivid. Between those areas 
and the main arch, from 7:20 p.m. to 7:35 p.m., rather faint 
streamers of light formed in the west, passing across the sky to 
the east, the passage occupying about six minutes. The main arch 
rose steadily, reaching a maximum at 9:00 p.m. about 20 degrees 
south of the zenith. By 9:30 p.m. the arch had begun to recede and 
the white areas to fade, and by 10:00 p.m. the colors of the arch 
began to change to white. When last seen, at 11:10 p.m., the arch, 
which had become dim, had receded to about 25 degrees above the 
north horizon.		E.W.H.
