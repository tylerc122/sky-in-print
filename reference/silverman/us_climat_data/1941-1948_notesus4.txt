Total, 1941-1948:

The volume including data for April to June 1944 is not in the 
AFRL Research Library.

Note that with 1937 most states have now stopped giving specific 
locations of auroral observations. The trend had started earlier, 
e.g., with North Dakota. For 1941-1948 only occasional reports of 
specific sites are found.

Beginning with January 1943 both Montana and North Dakota stopped 
noting auroral activity altogether. Possibly this was because of 
manpower shortage caused by the war, and saving of time in 
abstracting and reporting large amounts of data, but this is only 
a speculation on my part. South Dakota followed suit in June of 
1943. In October 1943 Wisconsin began tabulating the dates of 
auroral occurrence and the number of stations reporting for each 
date tabulated.

During this period I have again relied largely on GNIS for 
geographic coordinates of sites not previously listed. Where the 
site is not listed on GNIS, but where a Post Office address is 
given in Climatological Data, I have used coordinates of the Post 
Office. Where neither the site nor a Post Office address is given, 
but the county has been listed in Climatological Data (true almost 
always) I have averaged the latitudes and longitudes of the 
extremes of the coordinates for populated places given in GNIS, or 
used the coordinates for the county given in the Getty Thesaurus 
of Geographic Names. 

There is a minimum of activity in 1942 and 1943, especially in the 
first months of 1943. Even taking account of the lower level of 
reporting from North Dakota and Montana the level seems to be even 
lower than in the period immediately following the prolonged solar 
activity minimum of the early 1900s.

		Iowa: On the night of the 18th one of the most brilliant 
and beautiful auroral displays in many years was observed in all 
parts of the State. The display began early in the evening and 
continued until near midnight. At its height the lights and 
streamers covered about four-fifths of the sky and the glow 
extended southward until the southern edge was only about 20o above 
the southern horizon. The brilliance was described by some 
observers as being equal to that of the moon when it is about 
three-quarters full. This office has received a number of 
excellent detailed descriptions of the phenomenon made by 
cooperative observers who carefully noted changing aspects of the 
aurora throughout the display. However, space does not permit 
publication of these reports and they are being filed for future 
reference and study. The auroral disturbance caused some 
interruption of telephone, telegraph and radio for short periods. 
Auroras were also observed on the 10th, 11th, 17th, 19th, 20th and 
27th, but did not approach the display of the 18th in brilliance.
