# Middleburg, Fla., Jan 1888: trail notes

## Clay County Archives reply (2026-10-22, Vishi Garig, Historical Archives Supervisor)
- The archives already knew the story: Garig's 2020 column "This Month in Clay History: Fire in the Sky". **She never
  found what happened to the stone.** The Clay County trail ends where ours does: the steamer *Twilight*, the
  Sub-Tropical Exposition and the Proctor samples.
- **New details from her article.** Her sources aren't stated; probably the same papers we have.
  - A search party was led by A. S. Chalker.
  - The stone splintered a large pine and was buried "eight feet" in the middle of a road.
  - Pieces of about 200 and about 15 lb.
  - It went up Black Creek to Jacksonville on the *Twilight*. The archives hold a photo of the steamer.
- **The families:**
  - The Chalkers and Budingtons are pioneer families with descendants still in the area, and the archives hold a lot
    on both.
  - The Clark-Chalker house on Main St. (1830s) is the county's oldest building.
  - Albert Chalker owned property off Main St.
- The attachments (articles and family photos) are kept outside the repo. They are the archive's material and not
  ours to republish.

## What this changes
- **Not a new discovery locally.** The story is known in Clay County (2020) and on hobbyist forums. What we add is
  the set of contemporary sources: 12 newspaper items, Dec 1887 to Mar 1888.
- **Name correction:** two copies give the farm owner as "W. G. Burlington". We read this as **Budington**, the
  pioneer landowning family of Middleburg. OCR and typesetting garble names in this corpus all the time. The initials
  "W. G." are unverified: Ozias Budington died in 1886, so it would be a son or other relative.
- **The open question is the same: what happened at the Exposition?** The Jacksonville Historical Society and UF
  inquiries are the ones that can answer it.

## Resolved (2026-10-22): limonite, not a meteorite
- **G. F. Kunz** (Tiffany & Co.; a leading meteorite authority of the time), at the New York Academy of Sciences,
  Feb 1888: "the supposed meteorite, exhibited at the Florida Sub-tropical Exposition, weighing two hundred pounds,
  and said to have fallen at Middleburg, Florida, was only a mass of limonite." *Transactions of the N.Y. Academy of
  Sciences* 7:135 (1888). Archive.org: `transactionsofne78788newy`.
- *Science* (1888), "Diamonds in Meteorites", repeats it: the stone exhibited at the Subtropical Exposition "is a
  concretionary limonite".
- **Charles Fort retold it in *The Book of the Damned* (1919, p. 67):** "In Science, March 9, 1888, we read of a
  block of limestone, said to have fallen near Middleburgh, Florida ... The writer, in Science, denies that it fell
  from the sky."
  - Fort garbled limonite into "limestone" and framed the verdict as scientific dogma.
  - The forum versions ("200-pound block of limestone") come from Fort.
- **This fits the caution flagged earlier.** "Iron ore covered with white crystals" was Florida ironstone, and the
  thunderstorm fall was the classic thunderstone story.
- **How new is this? Not very.** The verdict was printed within weeks (Science, 9 Mar 1888; NYAS minutes, Feb 1888)
  and stood, garbled, in Fort's widely read book for a century. What we add is modest:
  - the local archive's 2020 article ends "we may never know" and does not have it;
  - Kunz's original wording ("only a mass of limonite") is clearer than Fort's version, and nothing online connects
    them.
- **Still unknown:** where the stone went after the Exposition. For local history that is the more interesting part.
