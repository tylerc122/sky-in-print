# Second independent audit — 2026-10-26

**Result: one strong record-correction candidate, two medium specimen-tracing leads, and a long weak tail. No newly confirmed unrecorded meteorite.** The strongest result is Lombard: a photograph and an explicit 1941 discovery narrative were published in 1951, whereas the catalogue gives 1953. The most useful negative result is the contemporary investigation rejecting the Kenyon recovery story.

## Evidence and checks

### 1. Lombard, Montana: discovery before the catalogued 1953 year

**Date/place:** Jesse Day reportedly found the object near Lombard in **1941**. Jesse Green's “Fragment From Another World,” *Montana Farmer-Stockman*, **1951-12-01, p. 11**, includes its photograph. **Grade: strong correction candidate**, not a new meteorite.

**Key quote, checked against the scan:** “IN 1941 JESSE DAY (deceased) found a heavy rock in the vicinity of Lombard.” [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86075096/1951-12-01/ed-1/?sp=11).

The article traces the object to mineral enthusiast **George Shaw's shoe shop in Bozeman**, says investigation identified it as a meteorite, describes a 15-pound iron-nickel mass with an end cut for analysis, and reports arrangements to send it to the **School of Mines museum at Butte**. That last transfer is prospective, not proven completed. Its claim that this was Montana's only identified meteorite is unreliable; that error does not erase the dated photograph and named ownership chain.

**Catalogue:** at the Lombard catalogue position, the sole snapshot entry within 60 km is **Lombard**, 7,000 g, Iron IIAB, **Found 1953**. No same-year falls within 400 km for 1941; also none for 1951 or 1953. Fifteen pounds is approximately 6.8 kg, consistent with, but not sufficient to prove, identity with the 7 kg catalogue mass.

**Literature:** the indexed [Meteoritical Bulletin entry, code 14679](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=14679) and [AMNH collection list](https://www.amnh.org/research/physical-sciences/earth-and-planetary-sciences/geology-collections/history-lists-meteorites-tektites-impactites/list-meteorites-name/amnh-meteorite-collection-i-l) give 1953. The Western Australian museum catalogue instead calls 1953 the recognition year and records a 7.2 kg total and a section obtained from H. H. Nininger. This distinction may explain the inherited date, but the newspaper already identifies the object in 1951. [Museum catalogue, Lombard entry](https://museum.wa.gov.au/sites/default/files/CATALOGUE%20OF%20WESTERN%20%20AUSTRALIAN%20METEORITE%20COLLECTIONS.pdf). That catalogue wording was available in indexed text; the full PDF download failed. Buchwald (1975), vol. 2, p. 782, is the specific further history to inspect; its linked archive PDF could not be retrieved, so I do **not** claim to have checked its narrative. IA full-text query: `"Lombard" "meteorite"`; no additional matching discovery history verified from those results.

**Forward check:** five-year search for Lombard / Jesse Day / George Shaw; supplementary article-text search through 1964. No correction of this story found. Several apparent hits are OCR “lombard” for *bombard*.

**Next decisive check:** compare the 1951 photograph and Day–Shaw provenance with the Nininger history and surviving Lombard sections. If identical, record **found 1941, publicly described by December 1951**; retain Found status. Even if the newspaper's retrospective 1941 year is mistaken, its 1951 publication is incompatible with discovery in 1953. Check **Lombard** by hand in the live database and its underlying catalogue references before proposing a formal amendment.

### 2. Ashley / Sauk Centre, Minnesota: school custody, with a weaker fall inference than the earlier summary suggests

**Date/place:** H. N. Baldwin's farm, **section 33 in Ashley**; found **November 15, 1899**, with November 9 inferred as the fall date. **Grade: medium for tracing a specimen; unverified as a meteorite.** This is the **Ashley** item already listed in `sweep_leads_1889_1913.md`, not an additional independent fall.

**Key quote:** “Prof. Stanton went out and brought it into the city” and “It will be submitted to the state university for examination.” *Sauk Centre Herald*, **1899-11-30, p. 1**, “A Splendid Specimen.” [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn89064489/1899-11-30/ed-1/?sp=1).

Direct image inspection matters here: the print reads **section 33**, not OCR “80,” and **15th**, not OCR “18th.” Byron F. Carr and Baldwin found a roughly ovoid, 27-pound, corroded-iron-looking object in disturbed ground. The article explicitly says the observers **did not see it strike the ground**. The earlier shorthand “a dozen witnesses” must not be turned into a dozen witnesses to stone recovery or ground impact.

**New custody evidence:** the [December 7 school notes, p. 4](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn89064489/1899-12-07/ed-1/?sp=4) say Stanton obtained it and exhibited it in the different grades. [December 28 pupil accounts, p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn89064489/1899-12-28/ed-1/?sp=1) repeat the object and university interest; one gives 29 pounds. These corroborate an object being shown at school, not its extraterrestrial origin. A [December 1 reprint, p. 7](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn89064526/1899-12-01/ed-1/?sp=7) uses Raymond as a locality; do not silently geocode this to the separate town of Raymond far to the south.

**Catalogue:** no snapshot entry within 60 km of the provisional Ashley-area anchor; no 1899 fall within 400 km. Nearest listed objects include Anoka, about 138 km, and Arlington, about 154 km. The actual section should be located on a historical plat before using these as precise distances.

**Literature and forward check:** IA `"Sauk Centre" "meteorite"`, targeted web searches including Stanton, and a name search in Ward's 1904 catalogue yielded no verified identification of this object. Minnesota survey and almanac hits often merely contain both search terms elsewhere in the volume. A potentially useful 1954 Minnesota mineral guide could not be downloaded. Five-year forward search found school reports but no university verdict or explicit debunk.

**Objection and next step:** “rough, spongy surface” and corroded iron are a substantial **slag warning**. Crude iron detection proves little. The economical next record is the school/university accession or correspondence trail under **Ashley, Sauk Centre/Sauk Center, Baldwin, Carr, Stanton**. Do not infer that the announced university transfer occurred.

### 3. Salisbury Beach, Massachusetts: a reported Harvard submission in 1934

**Date/place:** **September 26, 1934**, Black Rock area, Salisbury Beach. Leonard Murphy, an ERA timekeeper, and a work crew were associated with the recovery claim. **Grade: medium.**

**Key quote:** “Only one fragment was recovered, that at Salisbury Beach.” The continuation describes a pitted, irregular, fused-looking stony-metallic object. *Waterbury Democrat*, **1934-09-27**, [p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82014085/1934-09-27/ed-1/?sp=1) and [p. 4](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82014085/1934-09-27/ed-1/?sp=4). The p. 4 print was visually checked. An [AP account in the Evening Star, September 27, p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1934-09-27/ed-1/?sp=1) is another publication of the recovery claim, not another recovered stone.

The reports describe roughly a one-pound object, about seven by five inches, landing about 100 feet away and allegedly too hot to pick up for a few minutes. The dimensions, low reported mass, and heat claim all deserve scrutiny. The surrounding claims of numerous simultaneous local impacts are not independently established recoveries.

**Custody/literature:** an [Indianapolis Times editorial, October 2, p. 11](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015313/1934-10-02/ed-1/?sp=11), says an ERA worker took the object to Shapley. [TIME, October 8, “Science: Meteors”](https://time.com/archive/6767454/science-meteors/), also reports its acceptance for analysis. These support a submission trail; neither is a laboratory identification. IA `"Salisbury Beach" "meteorite"` and targeted searches did not produce a verified subsequent verdict.

**Catalogue:** no entry within 60 km of the beach anchor; no 1934 fall within 400 km. Walnut Hill (1978, about 119 km), Northampton (1963, about 160 km), and Nobleborough (1823, about 175 km) are different dated records, not automatic matches.

**Forward check:** five-year Salisbury/Murphy search, with full-article checks to eliminate unrelated Murphy mentions. No analytical result or explicit debunk located. Check **Salisbury, Salisbury Beach, Black Rock, Murphy** in live MBDB, including doubtful/unapproved names. Harvard Observatory/Shapley correspondence and specimen intake records are the useful next trail. A museum's acceptance of a submitted stone is not acceptance of its identification.

### 4. Manchester, Virginia: Gallager's stone intended for Dr. Taylor

**Date/place:** Manchester, now part of Richmond; a Sunday night before publication on **October 31, 1876**. The exact Sunday is not established from the reprint. **Grade: weak.**

**Key quote:** “It will be turned over to Dr. Taylor of this city for analysis.” *Bristol News*, **1876-10-31, p. 1**, citing the *Richmond Whig*. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85026955/1876-10-31/ed-1/?sp=1).

Ed **Gallager** reportedly watched a stone fall near his feet, waited for it to cool, then collected a blackened, iron-ore-like object almost two inches across. The analyst's city is the quoted Richmond source's “this city,” not necessarily Bristol. The proposed transfer and hot-stone language are not evidence of a completed analysis.

**Catalogue:** **Richmond**, LL5, 1,800 g, Fell **1828**, is about **8 km** from the anchor. No 1876 fall within 400 km. This precludes saying “no nearby recognized meteorite.” It does not itself make the 1876 account a Richmond fragment: require evidence of recycling, rediscovery, or specimen provenance.

**Literature/forward:** IA `"Manchester" "meteorite" "1876"`, targeted web searches, and Ward 1904 name search: no matching identification verified. Five-year search on Manchester/Gallager/Gallagher found no Taylor verdict. Generic place-name and shipping hits were not counted as follow-ups. Manual names: **Manchester, Richmond, Gallager/Gallagher**. The next useful source is the original *Whig* item or Taylor's correspondence; until then this is a weak recovery report, not a correction to Richmond 1828.

### 5. Freedom, Missouri: Joseph Younge household, July 9, 1870

**Key quote:** “Its weight is about as much as a man can readily lift.” *Bloomfield Times*, **1870-08-02, p. 4**. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84026050/1870-08-02/ed-1/?sp=4). An [August 4 Democratic Press reprint, p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83035083/1870-08-04/ed-1/?sp=1) attributes the report to the *Lexington, Mo., Caucasian*. **Grade: weak.**

The letter says the family of **Joseph Younge** saw a luminous, noisy phenomenon on July 9; a dark, irregular, metallic-looking body was found about 200 yards from the house. A bright metallic spot, sonorous ring, and elasticity are described. These are more useful than a bare “meteor fell” notice but do not establish nickel-bearing meteoritic metal. The report does not provide a continuing collection trail.

**Place check:** “Freedom, Missouri” is not an adequate unique location. A first Osage County anchor is uncertain. The State Historical Society documents a historical **Freedom settlement in Lafayette County**, with a post office by 1853, making it a plausible alternative given the Lexington source. [Historical place-name entry](https://collections.shsmo.org/manuscripts/columbia/C2366/lafayette-county). This supports an alternative, not a proven relocation of the farm.

**Catalogue:** neither the provisional Osage anchor nor an alternative near Aullville/Freedom Township, Lafayette County, has a snapshot meteorite within 60 km or an 1870 fall within 400 km. At the Osage anchor, Little Piney (1839) is only about **61.5 km** and Warrenton (1877) about **64.7 km** away: a marginal “outside 60” result is particularly unsafe with an uncertain farm location. The geographic exclusion remains provisional.

**Literature/forward:** IA queries `"Freedom" "meteorite" "Missouri"` and `"Younge" "meteor"`, web searches for the named household/source, and Ward 1904 name search found no verified identification. Five-year Freedom/Younge search found the reprint but no verified debunk or later specimen destination. Manual names: **Freedom, Lafayette County, Osage County, Joseph Young/Younge**. Resolve the correspondence locality before classifying this as unrecorded.

### 6. Pasquotank County, North Carolina: Vaughan's fair exhibit

**Date/place:** New Bern fair, held shortly before **March 11, 1898**; object said to have fallen in **Pasquotank County**, at an unspecified date. **Grade: weak.**

**Key quote:** “a meteorite that fell in Pasquotank county.” *Fisherman & Farmer*, **1898-03-11, p. 5**, a report of Mrs. Hargett's collection citing the *New Berne Journal*. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn91068084/1898-03-11/ed-1/?sp=5).

The lender is **Mr. Vaughan**; his initial is unclear in the OCR and should not be fixed by guesswork. The same contribution includes purported petrified human organs. That is a warning about the exhibit's standards, not proof that this particular object was false. The collection draws on Elizabeth City and Edenton contributors; New Bern is the exhibition site, not the claimed fall site.

**Catalogue:** no entry within 60 km of a central Pasquotank anchor; Norfolk, Found 1907, is about 71 km away. **A same-year comparison is impossible because no fall year is given.** As a conservative substitute, all snapshot falls within 400 km dated on or before 1898 were enumerated: Castalia 1874 (~164 km), Richmond 1828 (~174), Cross Roads 1892 (~183), Nanjemoy 1825 (~253), Caswell County 1810 (~270), and Monroe 1849 (~400, boundary-sensitive). None is thereby identified with Vaughan's object. This is not a passed same-year exclusion.

**Literature/forward:** IA `"Pasquotank" "meteorite"`; the [1909 bibliography of North Carolina geology](https://archive.org/details/bibliographyofno00laneiala) was checked in full text for the locality, with no matching fall description found. Five-year Pasquotank/Vaughan/Hargett search found no subsequent verdict or disposition. Manual names: **Pasquotank, Elizabeth City, Edenton, Vaughan**, plus the known regional names above. Look first for fair entry/catalogue records and Vaughan's collection provenance; this is an exhibition lead, not an established fall.

### 7. Orlando, Florida: J. K. Duke's store, October 1886

**Date/place:** Orlando; fireball roughly two weeks before the **October 21, 1886** report; a later Saturday garden discovery. Exact event dates are uncertain because this is a reprinted local item. **Grade: weak.**

**Key quote:** “stone or piece of metallic lava weighing half pound, which may be seen at Mr. Dukes store.” *Savannah Morning News*, **1886-10-21, p. 6**. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015137/1886-10-21/ed-1/?sp=6).

J. K. Duke reportedly watched a fireball apparently approach his yard. Mrs. Duke later picked up the object in the garden. The paper expressly says no definite origin had been established. This is an identifiable shop-display trail, but the later find is only associated with the earlier light by inference. “Metallic lava” could describe slag.

**Catalogue:** **Orlando**, Eucrite, Fell 2004, is about 2 km away; **Eustis**, H4, Found 1918, 502 g, about 44 km. No 1886 fall within 400 km. The modern witnessed Orlando fall is chronologically separate. Eustis requires a provenance comparison; a different date/weight alone is not sufficient to rule out an old find moving through collections.

**Literature/forward:** IA `"Orlando" "meteorite" "Duke"` and `"Orlando" "meteorite" "1886"`, plus targeted web searches, produced no verified identification of Duke's object. The IA results include whole-volume co-occurrences, not a new scientific description. Five-year Orlando/Duke follow-up search and supplemental nearby-name/meteor-text checks found no verified verdict; yacht, racehorse and unrelated Florida items account for much noise. Manual names: **Orlando, Eustis, Duke, Orange County**. Probably a terrestrial object remains a live explanation; retain only as a low-priority shop-provenance inquiry.

### 8. Diamond Valley, Nevada: an available fragment under a sensational parent-mass story

**Date/place:** purported fall about **1885**, described as seven years earlier in **1892**; Diamond Valley, Eureka County. **Grade: weak.**

**Key quote:** “The small piece, weighing about six ounces, can be seen at the Arlington House.” *Morning Appeal*, Carson City, **1892-01-29, p. 3**. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86076999/1892-01-29/ed-1/?sp=3).

**George L. Dobbs**, a school janitor and former miner, reportedly gave a reporter the fragment. **George Sheppard** and a companion were said to have witnessed the original fall. The parent object is described as 18 inches across, about 800 pounds, buried two feet, and too hot to handle. Those unverified figures and the seven-year retrospective story substantially weaken the claim. School employment does not mean the specimen entered a school collection.

**Catalogue/literature:** the snapshot has **Diamond Valley**, H5, 440 g, Found **2001**, about 48 km from the provisional southern-valley anchor; no 1885 fall within 400 km. The [official entry](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.cfm?code=7641) describes the 2001 find, not this story. Primary [Cascadia laboratory material](https://meteorites.pdx.edu/pubs/1690.pdf) also identifies later Diamond Valley 002 and 003 meteorites absent from this old snapshot. Name collision is not identity. IA `"Diamond Valley" "meteorite"` and `"Dobbs" "meteorite"` yielded no verified identification; an apparent American Naturalist hit concerned a different Diamond Valley and was rejected after reading the text.

**Forward check:** five-year Diamond Valley/Sheppard/Dobbs/Eureka search, followed by examination of relevant text windows; no verified debunk or laboratory result. Manual names: **Diamond Valley and numbered variants, Eureka, Dobbs, Sheppard**. Do not merge the fragment with a later Eureka-area museum stone without documentary evidence. A terrestrial or invented story is quite possible; the only concrete attraction is the stated six-ounce display fragment.

### 9. Surrounded Hill, Arkansas: short forty-pound recovery claim

**Date/place:** near Surrounded Hill, Prairie County, “last week” in a **June 21, 1879** report. **Grade: weak.**

**Key quote:** “a large meteoric stone fell near Surrounded Hill, Prairie county, Arkansas, last week, which weighed forty pounds.” *Memphis Appeal*, **1879-06-21, p. 2**. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045160/1879-06-21/ed-1/?sp=2).

A stated weight suggests someone claimed to have a physical object, but there is no named finder, observer, analyst, or collection. This is less useful than a specimen with a custody trail.

**Catalogue:** **Slovak**, H5, 8,220 g, Found **1962**, is about **36 km** from the provisional locality. No 1879 fall within 400 km. A much later find can represent an earlier fall, so the date difference does not clear Slovak; conversely there is no evidence linking these objects.

**Literature/forward:** IA `"Surrounded Hill" "meteorite"`; the [1894 Arkansas geological survey volume](https://archive.org/details/annualreportgeo00survgoog) was searched in full text. Its locality and meteorite references did not supply a matching history. Five-year Surrounded/Prairie County follow-up search found no verified identification or debunk. Manual names: **Surrounded Hill, Prairie County, Slovak**. Keep below all named-holder leads; the brief report alone is inadequate to claim either an unrecorded fall or a Slovak date correction.

### 10. Big Hole, Montana: Newkirk cabinet trail, probably a non-meteorite story

**Date/place:** Gibbons battlefield vicinity, Big Hole country; a Sunday storm in June 1883, reported in the **June 23** paper. Do not assign a precise fall day from the reprinted relative date. **Grade: weak; probably terrestrial lava-like material or a fabricated encounter.**

**Key quote:** “The gentlemen brought in several pieces … [one] will be placed in George Newkirk's cabinet.” *Butte Weekly Miner*, **1883-06-23, p. 1**. [LOC scan](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84036033/1883-06-23/ed-1/?sp=1). The bracketed word repairs uncertain OCR; the promised destination is clear, but completed deposition is not established.

Joe Summerfield and a companion whose surname is rendered **Wealthersby/Weathersby** described a 500–600-pound, truncated-conical, lava-like mass, hot and sulfurous, with a greenish crust and holes. The thunderstorm, spectacular descent, weight, heat and material description together are poor evidence for a meteorite. A [Livingston Enterprise comment, June 25, p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85053382/1883-06-25/ed-1/?sp=1), already says the account has elements of improbability and should be taken “cum grano salis.” This is contemporary skepticism, not a laboratory examination.

**Catalogue:** no entry within 60 km of the battlefield anchor and no 1883 fall within 400 km. Hayden Creek, Iron IIIAB, Found 1895, is about 76 km away; proximity alone supplies no link.

**Literature/forward:** IA `"Big Hole" "meteorite"`, `"Newkirk" "meteorite"`, and targeted web searches found no verified scientific identification. Five-year forward search located reprints and local skepticism, not a confirming examination. Manual names, if pursued: **Big Hole, Gibbons battlefield, Newkirk, Summerfield, Hayden Creek**. Do not spend specimen-recovery effort on this ahead of ranks 1–3; the cabinet mention is the reason it remains on the list at all.

## How this audit was done, and what remains unproved

Read `docs/SPEC.md` and `results/PILOT_REPORT.md` first, then the fall reports, previous audit and follow-up, and outreach log. Existing files were treated as hypotheses to test. No email service was accessed and no messages were sent. Existing results were not edited and nothing was committed.

The local work/sweep corpus was joined by `file#article_id` to the existing model labels. The temporary index contains **273,557 unique article records**. Selection used article text as well as labels, including articles labelled `none`, `fireball`, other categories, or doubtful. Label names are not judgments of truth; “none” can still contain a useful accession notice, and a `fireball` article can contain the decisive debunk of a `meteorite_fall` article.

Specific coverage:

- Ran `python -m skyinprint.fireball_falls 2` and examined the meteor/recovery passages of **all 128 family-first records with exactly two publication cities**. These are queue records, not 128 independent falls. Long articles were read in relevant context, not necessarily every unrelated column.
- A broader text screen generated **32,307 candidate article records** outside the accepted observed-fall subset. These were a screening pool, **not 32,307 manually read articles**.
- A focused collection/destination screen produced **1,731 distinct text windows**, 1,144 outside the accepted-fall subset. A refined set of **384 unique article excerpts** was read, followed by full relevant passages for selected leads. Read the 21 doubtful `meteorite_fall` records in meteor-related context and additional high-scoring records. The filters favor named people and collection trails and can still miss terse or badly OCR'd reports.
- Checked the **actual scan** for Lombard's date/photograph, Ashley's full November 30 notice, the Salisbury September 27 continuation, and the Kenyon rejection. Other newspaper quotations here are from local OCR, with spacing and line-break hyphens normalized; their scans are linked for verification. Uncertain names/dates are explicitly left uncertain.
- For shortlisted claims, computed great-circle distances against `reference/metbull/Meteorite_Landings.csv`, inspected **all entries within 60 km**, and **same-year Fell entries within 400 km**. Anchors are approximate localities, not inferred impact coordinates. Unknown dates and uncertain localities are disclosed rather than treated as negative matches.
- Ran the project's forward-search helper for selected names with a **60-month argument**. Its implementation actually uses `31 × months` days, so the searched interval is about 61 calendar months. Each lead's interpretation above distinguishes a relevant result from unrelated matches. No debunk found means only none found in the available keyword-selected corpus during that interval.
- Ran `skyinprint.ia_fulltext` queries for the named leads, plus targeted web literature searches. IA searches can match different pages of a book, return only a first page of results, and admit undated modern items despite the nominal historical filter. I checked plausible hits in context; I did **not** interpret the number of returned hits as independent evidence. Particularly misleading hits in the Minnesota surveys, Arkansas survey, *American Naturalist*, and *Mineralogical Magazine* were discarded when the locality reference did not describe the claimed meteorite.

### Two methodological problems exposed by the audit

**The forward helper can hide the relevant passage.** It takes the first occurrence of the place/person regex and prints only 400 characters before and 600 after it, while merely requiring a meteor keyword somewhere in the whole article. With a broad name such as Jackson or Murphy, the printed excerpt can be an unrelated earlier paragraph. Its debunk flag is computed on that same window. I supplemented this by searching all name occurrences near meteor text and reading relevant full passages. The Amis/Jackson source itself illustrates the problem: the useful stone description can lie well away from the first “Jackson.” **An unflagged helper hit is not evidence that an article contains no debunk.** No source code was changed.

**Two publication cities do not supply two witnesses.** Wire copy, borrowed local notices and recycled anecdotes frequently explain the queue's city count. A named school display or museum submission is evidence about an object's custody; it does not independently validate the original fall story. Conversely, the presence of a proposed institutional destination must not be upgraded to a completed transfer or laboratory confirmation.
