How I did it

A computer read the papers, I read the good bits, and then I chased every claimed fall through later papers, journals, museum records and a lot of emails to small-town historical societies.

The method

01

Find the reports

Keyword patterns search the OCR text of American Stories, a research group's transcription of the Library of Congress newspaper scans. A model running on my own machine (Flint) sorts the actual sky reports from everything else. All it does is narrow the pile, it doesn't decide whether anything came from space. Pilot report

02

Read them

Reports get grouped, reprints get compared, and I read the interesting ones myself, with extra review on the biggest fireballs. A paper's hometown isn't always where the witness was, and lots of papers reprinted the same account, so a dozen clippings can be one person. Grouping and review

03

Follow what happened next

Every claimed fall gets chased forward through later papers, science journals and museum records. Sometimes a retraction settles it. Sometimes a named museum or library turns an old story into a hunt for an actual object. None of the open leads is a confirmed new meteorite. The thirteen cases

What the map can't tell you

More dots usually means more newspapers

How many papers got digitized swings a lot by year and state, and reprints and OCR quality move the dots around too, so the map shows what got printed and survived, which has little to do with how often meteors fell. The timeline has a separate line for newspaper titles per year from the sweep, and a missing year means I don't know. Article counts are in coverage.json.

Two kinds of pin

Fall cases sit at the rough place the papers named. Each fireball group sits at one publishing town, the one closest to the other towns that printed it, so those pins are where papers were and have nothing to do with where anything landed or where people stood. Fireball pins get bigger with the number of towns, which is only a rough stand-in for how many separate people saw it. Field definitions

It's not everything

The map has fireball groups printed in at least two towns, plus anything I flagged by hand. Some of those groups probably still mix up unrelated sky reports. Dates are only as exact as the papers were, and 1770 to 1964 is just the range I searched, with plenty of gaps inside it. Selection and limits

How much it misses

Five of seven known falls

I tested the search on the 1870s and it found five of the seven recorded U.S. falls, 71%, with a wide 95% interval of 36 to 92%. A full-year rescan for the two it missed found nothing about them at all, so I think those papers just aren't in the collection, though OCR mangling them is possible too. Five for five on the ones that are in there is a good sign and that's all it is. Recovery test and rescan

A blind check changed the rules

I read 100 fall reports the ranker had scored low and found seven that named where the stone ended up, which it should have caught. I changed the rules after that. Since I tuned on that same sample, it can't double as proof the new rules work. Blind check

I make mistakes too

In a blind review of excerpts I agreed with the model on 22 of the 24 it decided, and four more I couldn't call either way. That's a small sample. Garbled OCR, clipped excerpts and good hoaxes still trip both of us up. The check · Audits and limits

What the outcomes mean

The paintings

Each case has a painting at the top, made in code one brushstroke at a time. They're all by the same made-up painter, somebody from the 1890s who gives the sky most of the canvas, works in short dry strokes and sticks to a small palette, so the thirteen hang together as a set.

Each one is built from that case's sources: the hour if it's known, the season, the ground, who was there, what people said they saw and heard. If the case was a hoax nothing falls in the picture, and if the papers don't give the hour or the season the caption says so. The same seed always gives the same painting.

The data

The release has the 13 cases, the fireball groups, source links and coverage numbers. CSV fields that hold lists are JSON. Quotes are short and keep the original OCR unless a fix is marked. It's a snapshot and won't update on its own.

Sources and credits

Newspaper scans come from the Library of Congress, Chronicling America. The text comes from American Stories (Dell et al.). The list of known meteorites comes from NASA's Meteorite Landings dataset and the Meteoritical Bulletin.

Other people have dug through old papers for fireballs before me. The Historical Fireballs project and the Nininger and Monnig collections shaped how I check sources, and I'm not claiming to be first.

Maps use US Atlas 3.0.1 from Census Bureau shapes, drawn with D3 7.9.0 and topojson-client 3.1.0. Type is Old Standard TT and Libre Franklin. Derived data is CC BY 4.0 and the code is MIT.
